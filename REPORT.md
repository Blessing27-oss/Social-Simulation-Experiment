## Part I: The Social Simulator's Dilemma

### Problem 1

This section evaluates whether the simulated "Agent Bank" is sufficiently powered to detect a human-like correlation of r = 0.3.

#### Part (a)

**Fisher Transformation**

The Fisher transformation of a correlation coefficient is:

$$
z_r = \tanh^{-1}(r) = \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{1+r}{1-r} \right)
$$

For r = 0.3:

$$
z_r = \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{1.3}{0.7} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \ln(1.857) \approx \frac{1}{2}(0.619) = 0.3095
$$

**Power Approximation**

Using z_1 -  α = 1.645 and z_1 - β= 0.84, the required sample size is:

$$
n \approx \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta}}{z_r} \right)^2 + 3 = \left( \frac{1.645 + 0.84}{0.3095} \right)^2 + 3 = (8.029)^2 + 3 = 67.46
$$

Therefore, a minimum of **n = 68** agents is required to achieve 80% power to detect a correlation of r = 0.3 at α = 0.05. This indicates that moderate correlations can be detected with a relatively modest sample size.

#### Part (b)

Some social-simulation studies use approximately N = 1000 agents.

Since only 68 agents are required for 80% power, a sample of 1000 agents is substantially more than necessary for detecting r = 0.3. However, such a large sample would allow researchers to detect much smaller correlations with high precision. Thus, while sufficient, the design would be statistically overpowered for this specific effect size.

### Problem 2

Agents must coordinate with heterogeneous partners who may be either a team player (P) or a free rider (F). We observe the sequence:

D = (Contribute, Fail, Contribute)

#### Part (a): Posterior Computation

**Priors:** P(P) = P(F) = 0.5

**Likelihoods:**

- P(C | P) = 0.8, P(Fail | P) = 0.2
- P(C | F) = 0.2, P(Fail | F) = 0.8

Assuming conditional independence:

$$
P(D \mid P) = 0.8 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.128
$$

$$
P(D \mid F) = 0.2 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.032
$$

Applying Bayes' Theorem:

$$
P(P \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid P)\,P(P)}{P(D \mid P)\,P(P) + P(D \mid F)\,P(F)} = \frac{0.128 \times 0.5}{(0.128 \times 0.5) + (0.032 \times 0.5)} = \frac{0.064}{0.080} = 0.8
$$

The posterior probability that the partner is a team player is **0.8**. The observed evidence substantially increases confidence relative to the prior of 0.5, indicating strong support for cooperative behavior.

#### Part (b)

Under a MAP decision rule, we contribute if P(P | D) > 0.5.

Since 0.8 > 0.5, the optimal decision is to **share data**. Despite one failure, the overall evidence favors cooperation.

### Problem 3

#### Part (a)

We apply the misclassification-adjusted estimator:

$$
\hat{\theta} = \frac{\hat{p} - (1 - q_0)}{q_1 + q_0 - 1}
$$

Given p̂ = 0.7, q₁ = 0.8, q₀ = 0.9:

$$
\hat{\theta} = \frac{0.7 - (1 - 0.9)}{0.8 + 0.9 - 1} = \frac{0.7 - 0.1}{0.7} = \frac{0.6}{0.7} \approx 0.857
$$

The bias-corrected fairness rate is approximately **85.7%**. This indicates that measurement error leads the raw estimate to understate the true fairness rate.

#### Part (b)

Because specificity (0.9) exceeds sensitivity (0.8), the classifier is more accurate at identifying unfair cases than fair ones. As a result, fair cases are more likely to be misclassified, leading to a downward bias in the observed fairness rate.

#### Part (c)

If sensitivity and specificity were unknown, they could be estimated using a gold-standard validation subsample labeled by human annotators. Comparing model predictions to human labels would allow empirical estimation of q₁ and q₀.

## Part II: Simulating the Social Dynamics of Science

## Problem 5 — Analysis Memo

### Methods

One hundred personas were generated by prompting an LLM (Gemini Flash) to write BFI-conditioned life stories and then administering the Big Five Inventory survey to score each persona on Openness (O), Conscientiousness (C), Extraversion (E), Agreeableness (A), and Neuroticism (N). Personas were randomly grouped into teams of four and each group ran through three multi-round game environments: a **Public Goods** game (6 rounds, endowment=10, threshold=24), a **Negotiation** game (4 rounds, total resource=100), and a **Collaboration** game (4 rounds, co-authorship framing). Each group was simulated under three context conditions that varied what persona information agents received: **life\_story\_only**, **bfi\_only**, and **life\_story\_and\_bfi**. Yielding 25 groups x 3 conditions x 3 scenarios.

Objective outcomes (contribution share, cooperation rate, fairness, welfare) were computed directly from actions. Open-ended rationales and utterances were additionally labeled by an LLM judge on five dimensions: judged fairness, cooperative intent, strategic constructiveness, leadership, and toxicity. Raw judge rates were bias-corrected using a hypothetical calibration with assumed sensitivity q1 = 0.88 and specificity q0 = 0.90 (not estimated from a real calibration dataset — an explicit pedagogical assumption in `conf/calibration/default.yaml`).

**Limitations.** The q1/q0 values are assumed, not empirically estimated, so corrected rates carry unchecked uncertainty. The same model family generates personas, acts as agents, and judges outputs, creating circularity, the judge may reward the kind of reasoning it was trained to produce. There is no human baseline for comparison; all condition differences are relative. Groups of four are small, and with 25 groups per condition the power to detect subtle trait effects is limited. Collaboration produces no objective reward, so its analysis relies entirely on the LLM judge. Finally, because the LLM "knows" what BFI trait labels mean semantically, the bfi\_only condition likely produces behavior anchored to label stereotypes rather than genuine personality expression.

---

### (a) Behavior Across Context Conditions and Environments

**Public Goods.** Life story conditions substantially outperformed BFI-only. Cooperation rates were 90.8% (life\_story\_only) and 92.3% (life\_story\_and\_bfi) versus only 74.0% for bfi\_only. Mean social welfare followed the same pattern: 29.7 and 30.3 versus 25.9. Adding BFI scores on top of life stories (life\_story\_and\_bfi) produced marginally higher cooperation than life story alone, but the difference is small relative to the gap with bfi\_only.

**Negotiation.** Differences were smaller but consistent in direction. BFI-only agents demanded more of the shared resource (mean demand share 0.273 vs ~0.252), resulting in the worst group welfare (-38.4 vs -35.1 and -33.5). Cooperation rates were similar across life story conditions (~53--59%) and bfi\_only (~59%), suggesting negotiation outcomes are less sensitive to context framing than public goods.

**Collaboration.** The largest gap appeared here, observable only through judged outcomes. BFI-only agents scored 0.48 on judged fairness and 0.51 on cooperative intent, near chance, while life story conditions scored 0.78--0.81 and 0.80--0.82 respectively. Combined life\_story\_and\_bfi produced the highest scores on all judged dimensions.

---

### (b) Objective Outcomes

Across both Public Goods and Negotiation, life story context conditions produced meaningfully better outcomes than BFI-only:

| Condition             | PG cooperation | PG welfare | Neg. welfare |
| --------------------- | -------------- | ---------- | ------------ |
| bfi\_only             | 74.0%          | 25.9       | -38.4       |
| life\_story\_only     | 90.8%          | 29.7       | -33.5       |
| life\_story\_and\_bfi | 92.3%          | 30.3       | -35.1       |

The public goods threshold (24 hours, requiring ~6 per agent) was consistently met under life story conditions; under bfi\_only, lower cooperation meant groups more often failed to reach the threshold, incurring catastrophe penalties. In negotiation, the reward structure (disagreement penalties that compound over rounds) penalised bfi\_only agents' more aggressive demands.

Trait-outcome correlations showed that Conscientiousness was the most consistently positive predictor of contribution (r = 0.28 in bfi\_only, 0.25 in life\_story\_and\_bfi), and Agreeableness was positively correlated in bfi\_only (r = 0.26). Openness showed a strong unexpected negative correlation in bfi\_only (r = -0.59), possibly an artifact of the BFI-label-to-behavior mapping rather than a genuine personality effect.

---

### (c) Judged Outcomes

After bias correction (q1 = 0.88, q0 = 0.90), judged fairness and cooperative intent tell a consistent story: life story conditions produce substantially more cooperative and fair-sounding rationales than BFI-only, most dramatically in Collaboration. For Public Goods, bias-corrected judged fairness was 0.91--0.93 under life story conditions versus 0.74 under bfi\_only. For Negotiation the gap narrowed (0.66--0.72 vs 0.66), consistent with the smaller objective differences observed there.

The life\_story\_and\_bfi condition consistently ranked highest on judged cooperative intent and strategic constructiveness, suggesting that combining narrative and trait information elicits the most prosocially articulate reasoning, even when objective contributions are similar to life\_story\_only.

---

### (d) Transcript Evidence and Mechanisms

Two mechanisms emerge from the dialogue logs:

**Mechanism 1 — Trait-label anchoring in bfi\_only.** Agents in the bfi\_only condition explicitly invoked their numeric trait scores in their rationales. For example, in Negotiation round 1: *"Given my low agreeableness and high conscientiousness, I am focused on efficiency and personal success rather than social harmony."* This suggests the LLM translates BFI labels directly into behavioural stereotypes (low agreeableness -> competitive; high conscientiousness -> task-focused but not prosocial), producing rigid, self-justifying behaviour that is less responsive to group dynamics.

**Mechanism 2 — Narrative-driven flexibility in life\_story\_only.** Agents given only a life story reasoned from concrete situational frames rather than trait labels. In the same Public Goods game: *"I view this cluster through the lens of cold survival. The threshold is 24 hours across the team; if it fails, my own work stalls. I will contribute the bare minimum necessary to ensure we meet the collective threshold."* Despite a similarly self-interested framing, this agent contributed 8/10, more than the BFI counterpart's 6/10, because the narrative grounded the decision in shared consequences rather than personal trait expression. Life story agents appear to reason instrumentally about group outcomes, whereas bfi\_only agents reason deontologically from their trait identity.

## Problem 6 — Prompt Intervention

### Intervention and Hypothesis

**Intervention.** One sentence was added to the agent system prompt in `conf/agent/llm_intervention.yaml`:

> *"Note: personality traits describe behavioral tendencies under typical conditions, not rigid scripts, context, incentives, and group dynamics always shape how these tendencies express in any given situation."*

The baseline system prompt instructs agents to "make decisions based on your background, personality profile, recent interaction history and incentives." This leaves the BFI-only condition vulnerable to trait-label anchoring: agents treat their scores as fixed behavioural rules rather than contextual tendencies.

**Hypothesis.** Adding explicit trait-as-tendency framing will reduce anchoring in the bfi\_only condition, causing those agents to weigh situational incentives more heavily. This should increase bfi\_only cooperation rates and narrow the gap with life story conditions, while having a smaller effect on life story conditions, where agents already reason situationally.

### Results

The intervention produced mixed evidence for the hypothesis. In **Collaboration, **where the trait-anchoring mechanism was most visible, the prediction held: bfi\_only judged fairness rose from 0.477 to 0.525 (+0.048) and cooperative intent from 0.507 to 0.535 (+0.028), while life\_story\_and\_bfi was essentially unchanged (-0.005). This is consistent with the intervention weakening stereotype anchoring specifically for agents who only have trait labels to reason from.

In **Public Goods**, the largest cooperation gain was unexpectedly in life\_story\_only (+4.2pp, from 90.8% to 95.0%), while bfi\_only improved only marginally (+1.5pp). In **Negotiation**, changes across all conditions were within noise (±0.5pp fairness, welfare changes under 2 units). The bfi\_only cooperation gap with life story conditions therefore narrowed in collaboration but not clearly in public goods or negotiation.

| Condition             | PG coop (baseline -> interv) | Collab judged fairness (baseline -> interv) |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| bfi\_only             | 74.0% -> 75.5% (+1.5pp)      | 0.477 -> 0.525 (+0.048)                     |
| life\_story\_only     | 90.8% -> 95.0% (+4.2pp)      | 0.775 -> 0.800 (+0.025)                     |
| life\_story\_and\_bfi | 92.3% -> 91.3% (-1.0pp)     | 0.812 -> 0.807 (-0.005)                    |

### Transcript Evidence

Despite the intervention, bfi\_only agents in Negotiation continued to invoke trait labels explicitly: *"Given my high conscientiousness and low agreeableness, I am focused on efficiency and securing what I need rather than social harmony."* The reasoning pattern is nearly identical to the baseline, suggesting that a single sentence in the system prompt is insufficient to override the strong prior the model has for translating BFI scores into behavioural stereotypes.

In Public Goods, the bfi\_only intervention agent reasoned: *"As a highly conscientious but low extraversion and low agreeableness individual... a contribution of 6 is a significant but fair share that ensures the goal is reached."* Compared to the baseline (*"I will contribute a proportional share"* with contribution 6), the framing is marginally more group-oriented but the numeric outcome is identical. The tendency framing appears to have shifted language without shifting action.

The larger lift for life\_story\_only in public goods may reflect that narrative agents are more sensitive to meta-level prompt instructions (they reason by story-building, so a new framing adds to the story), whereas bfi\_only agents are more anchored to the structural interpretation of trait scores regardless of softening language. This partially challenges the original hypothesis and suggests the anchoring mechanism may be more deeply embedded in how BFI label semantics interact with model priors than a single prompt-level instruction can address.
